A remote audit works if it is unscripted: you schedule the call, you name the stations while the camera is walking, and nobody gets a day's notice about the route. What a prepared video tour cannot prove is that the lot on the line is yours — so ask for the job traveller and the first-article photo to be shown on camera, with the revision visible.

## What a live call can prove, and what it cannot

**Can prove:**

- The equipment exists, is powered, and matches the capability claim (machine model readable on the frame)
- People are where the process says they should be, and they are doing the work described
- Materials, reels and boards are physically present in the stated condition
- A record can be produced and opened live, rather than described

**Cannot prove:**

- That the lot on the line is your lot
- That the process on that particular shift is the process on every shift
- That the yield figure someone quotes you is real
- That the plant will still be running your programme in eighteen months

Be honest with yourself about the boundary: a remote audit reduces risk, it does not certify a supplier. It is far better than nothing and considerably weaker than standing on the floor.

## The five things to ask the camera to show

Choose them while on the call. In this order, because each one depends on the previous being real:

| # | Ask for | What a fake looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Walk to the X-ray station — is someone reading an image on screen? | A machine visible, no operator, no screen lit, or a still image |
| 2 | The SPI screen with the job currently on the line | A generic dashboard, or "we can't show that" |
| 3 | The feeder cart for a live job, with a reel barcode visible | Reels present but no link to a program or a revision |
| 4 | The lot record for the batch on the floor, opened live | A template, a blank form, or a photo of a printed sheet |
| 5 | The coating booth, with masking prepared for a real board | A booth, no work in progress, no masking fixtures |

The pattern to watch for is not "no". It is **"that station is not running today"** repeated five times. Any single station can legitimately be idle. All five cannot.

## Records to have emailed during the call

Ask for these to be sent while you are still on the line — it tests responsiveness and keeps the audit from turning into a promise:

1. **First-article report** for a lot comparable to yours, with the revision it was built against
2. **AOI or X-ray images** from that lot, filed against the lot number
3. **Incoming bare-board inspection record** — thickness, warpage, finish, panel markings
4. **Component incoming check** — part number, quantity, date code, packaging integrity
5. **Certificate of conformance** as issued with a shipment

If the answer is "we will send it after the call", note how many days it takes. That turnaround is a preview of how the programme will run.

## Red flags on a prepared tour

- **A route that never leaves one aisle.** Eight lines cannot all be visible from one walkway.
- **Nobody working at the machine being shown.** Ask why, and listen for whether the answer names a real production condition.
- **A supervisor who answers every technical question and no operator who speaks.** The line knows things the office does not.
- **Reels with no labels, or labels facing away.** Reel-to-program binding is a real control; a tour that avoids it is avoiding the question.
- **Reluctance about the X-ray screen specifically.** X-ray is where voiding and head-in-pillow are read. It is also where a plant without a real inspection habit gets exposed.
- **"Our engineer is not available."** Then reschedule with the engineer. Do not proceed on a sales call.

## What to do after the call

Three things, same day, while it is fresh:

1. **Write down what you saw as facts and what you inferred separately.** The gap between them is your residual risk.
2. **Send a short list of follow-up questions** — the ones the call raised. Speed and specificity of the reply is a second data point about the supplier.
3. **Ask for one photo of a finished lot, packing stage**, with the label visible. It is a cheap test of whether photos actually get taken on that floor.

## How we run this

You pick the targets, we walk. No route is sent in advance and no station is pre-lit for the call. Typical things buyers ask for here: the X-ray screen with a BGA on it, the SPI result for the job running, the feeder cart with the reel barcode, the coating booth with masking in place, and the lot record for the batch on the floor.

Also on camera if you ask: a first-article photo from a past lot, the incoming bare-board check, and the machine program bound to a revision.

What we will say on the call, before you ask: in-circuit and functional test are not in the building, bare boards are bought rather than fabricated, and IATF 16949 / ISO 13485 / third-party IPC Class 3 are not held. If any of those is a requirement for your programme, the call has still been worth it — you found out in twenty minutes instead of at tooling.
