"PCBA since 2008" is on a thousand supplier profiles and it is almost never checked. That is odd, because it is the cheapest question in sourcing to verify and the one with the most direct bearing on whether a quote will still mean something in two years.

This note is about the check, not about us. It ends with our own answer, stated plainly, including the part of our history that gets misread.

## The date is a field on a document, and you can ask for the document

Every legally registered Chinese company has a business licence (营业执照) carrying four things a buyer can use: the registered name, the unified social credit code, the date of establishment, and the registered address. The date of establishment is not a marketing claim. It is printed by the registry.

So the first move is not to read an About page. It is to ask for the licence, then compare it against everything else the supplier has told you:

- Does the registered name match the certificate holder name, the bank beneficiary, and the quotation header?
- Does the registered address match the factory address you were given?
- Does the date of establishment match the "since" claim on the website and on third-party profiles?

Three mismatches out of three is a trading office. One or two is a conversation. This is the same document set the [supplier audit checklist](/insights/pcba-supplier-audit-checklist.html) is built around, and the date is the field people skip because it feels like trivia.

## What the date does not prove

Here is the part suppliers would rather you did not think about: **an old company can be a young assembler.** The two facts live in different places and there is no registry that records when a particular SMT line was installed.

Three ways the two diverge, all common:

1. **The entity is older than the plant.** A trading company or a design house that existed for years, then added assembly capacity later. The licence date is honest and the assembly experience is five years old.
2. **The owners restarted under a new entity.** The old company closed, the same people registered a new one, the website kept the original "since" year. You will find the year on the site and a recent establishment date on the licence.
3. **The company changed product, correctly and openly.** A business that genuinely did something else first and moved into assembly. Nothing dishonest — but the years spent on the earlier product are not years of assembly experience, and a buyer counting them is counting the wrong thing.

Our own case is the third one, and it is worth spelling out because an old profile of ours still causes exactly this misreading (see below).

## The check that actually separates them

Search history is weak evidence. Dated internal records are strong, because they are expensive to fake and cheap to sample.

**Ask for a record from a year you choose.** Not "your oldest first-article report" — that lets the supplier pick the best-looking document. Name a year — 2016, 2011 — and ask for a first-article report, a job traveller, or an inspection record from that year for a board of that type. Four outcomes:

| What comes back | What it means |
|---|---|
| A dated record matching the year you named | Strong. Someone kept an archive, and the archive reaches back. |
| A record from a different year, offered with an explanation | Fair. Ask why the year you named is missing before deciding. |
| Only certificates and photos | Weak. Photos carry no date that can be checked. |
| A promise to look and no follow-up | The answer is in the silence. |

Three more that cost one email each:

- **Customer references with dates attached**, not logos. "They have supplied us since 2019, here is the part number" is checkable by asking the reference. A wall of logos is not.
- **Equipment nameplates.** Every placer and reflow oven has a manufacturer plate with a serial. A 2023 machine on a floor that claims 2009 is not a scandal — lines get replaced — but the *mix* of ages tells you whether the floor has been running continuously or was built in one go.
- **The certificate, as issued rather than cropped.** The certificate number, the scope statement and the holder name are what a buyer checks against the issuing body. A cropped image is not a document.

## Our own case: the LED line, in the right order

Tenmay was founded on 2008-01-09 as a contract electronics assembly business. That has been the business continuously since.

At one point the company developed its own LED lighting product line and sold it under the ledsztm.com brand. It was a product the company made, sitting on top of the assembly business — not a change in what the company was. The line was later closed so the company could concentrate on assembly.

**The sequence is assembly, then an LED product line, then assembly only. It is not a move from lighting into electronics.**

This matters practically, because the misreading is easy to arrive at honestly. An old profile of ours on a B2B directory still lists the main category as lighting and still says the company has five years of experience — a sentence written more than a decade ago and never updated. A buyer who finds that page first, and reads it as the company's origin, will conclude the PCBA business is a reinvention. It is not. But you should not resolve that by taking our word for it, which is the whole point of the previous section: name a year and ask for a record from it.

What is still true today: LED *driver boards* are assembled here, as boards, for customers who build lighting products. Building a fixture and building the board inside it are different jobs, and we do the second one.

## What we claim, and what we do not

The useful half of a capability statement is the negative half.

**Held:** ISO 9001:2015, certified under GB/T 19001-2016, certificate 38822Q00487R0S. Note the edition: the certificate is issued against the 2015 edition, which ISO withdrew on 2026-09-16 in favour of ISO 9001:2026. Transition status sits with the certification body, and we say so rather than implying the current edition.

**Not held:** IATF 16949, ISO 13485, certified IPC Class 3 process, AS9100, AS9102 first-article reporting, NADCAP special processes, ITAR registration. Bare PCB fabrication is not done here — boards are bought from qualified partner fabs, and this is an assembly house. A programme that requires any of those is a programme we should decline early rather than quote and disappoint.

**Running today:** eight SMT lines, 300,000 placement points per day, from 01005 upward; through-hole and hand soldering; SPI, AOI and X-ray in line; conformal coating; device programming; minimum order 50 pieces, three working days standard once the kit is complete.

## One hour, in this order

1. Ask for the business licence. Check establishment date, credit code, registered address, registered name against the quotation header.
2. Ask for the ISO 9001 certificate as issued. Check the holder name against the licence and the scope against what you are buying.
3. Name a year and ask for a first-article report from it.
4. Ask one customer reference for a date and a part number.
5. Ask what the supplier does *not* hold. A supplier with a written negative list has thought about which programmes it should refuse.

None of that requires a flight, and it is the difference between buying years and buying a story. The date is worth exactly what your ability to check it is worth — so ask it of every supplier in the comparison, not just the one you already like.
