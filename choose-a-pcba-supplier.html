How to choose a PCBA supplier, board by board

Seventeen notes on the decision that actually costs money: whether one assembler builds the whole product or the build splits. Eleven of them take one board family and ask that question again, because the answer changes with the board rather than with the price list. The other six cover the checks that decide whether a quote is worth reading at all — what you are buying, whether the seller is the plant, which certificate your programme actually requires, and whether the founding date means what it looks like.

17 notes72 min end to endLast reviewed 18 Nov 2026

In 60 seconds

  1. 01One assembler usually wins when the boards share a failure mode — the inverter and the BMS on a power station, the three boards of a VFD that must ship on one date.
  2. 02Splitting is normal when the second board changes on a different clock: a console, a display, a keypad.
  3. 03Some splits are not a choice. Active lens alignment on a dashcam is not an assembly step, so no assembler can keep it in one house.
  4. 04On a safety-related board the first question is not unit price but whether one serial can be tied to one build lot.
  5. 05On a dense consumer board the requirement that decides who can quote is test coverage, not placement capability.

First: one supplier, two, or three?

The unit you buy is usually the product, not the board. These four notes work out where the unit actually is.

  • Quoting a VFD as three boards or as one set

    Why the set, not the board, is the unit — and why both pricing views are worth asking for.

  • Sharing a build stack between servo drives and VFDs

    When sharing a stack with a VFD is real leverage, and when it quietly hands your supplier pricing power.

  • Should one assembler build all three portable power station boards?

    The inverter decides output quality, so it is the board worth keeping next to the BMS.

  • Should you buy the e-bike controller and display from one assembler?

    Two boards on two revision clocks: split the build, but never split the harness drawing.

Second: who carries the risk?

Once the build is split, something unowned is left in the gap. These four notes name what it is on each family.

  • Where liability sits on a jump starter with an air compressor

    One failed switch can destroy a vehicle's electronics. That risk has to be written down, not assumed.

  • What an EVSE control board audit actually asks for

    On a safety-related board the first question is whether one serial can be tied to one lot.

  • Who owns the optics on a dashcam build?

    Lens alignment is not assembly, so no choice of assembler can keep it in one house.

  • Potting before test turns a defect into scrap

    Cheap board, irreversible potting: the order of the last two operations sets the scrap rate.

Third: what must stay with you?

Three notes where the honest answer is that a step belongs inside your own building — or must be bought in, not assembled.

  • Do you actually need in-circuit test on a robot vacuum mainboard?

    Specify ICT and most high-mix floors are out of the running. Know what you are buying before you do.

  • The 800 mm board is not the hard part of a treadmill build

    The 800 mm board is the easy question; a console harness in a sealed housing is not.

  • Buy a certified HMI module, or assemble the board yourself?

    The board is cheaper and the liability moves to you. On an isolation product that is the whole decision.
