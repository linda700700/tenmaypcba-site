Where the build risk sits, board by board

The build risk on a board family sits in one step, and naming that step changes what goes into the drawing and what a supplier is asked to prove. On a 1U rectifier it is the void under a power device, read on an X-ray image against a written limit. On an LED receiver card it is the ribbon header that gets cycled at install. On a rugged handheld it is the conformal coat that carries the IP rating. Twenty-three families, one step each, and the same question to put on the table before the price.

In 60 seconds

  1. 01On most of these families the inspection step carries more of the answer than the placement step, so it is the part of the quotation worth reading first.
  2. 02A joint hidden under a power device decides the efficiency figure. AOI reads the fillet; X-ray reads the void against a written limit.
  3. 03Where the failure is mechanical, a connector, a ribbon header, a motor tab or a flex through a hinge, assembly executes a drawing that was decided earlier, so that drawing is where the leverage sits.
  4. 04A coating, a masking step or a potting step changes what the board survives, and it closes the rework path once it is applied. The order of the last two operations belongs in the design.
  5. 05On six families the acceptance measurement happens in another building, and the arrangement for it belongs in writing before the first build.

The table that saves the reading

The notes, in the order they help

Ordered by decision, not by publication date. Read to the end of a group before jumping to the next one.

First: the joint under the part

Three families where the electrical result depends on a joint underneath the part, and where the inspection step is the interesting part of the quote.

  • A 1U rectifier board is a thermal part with a circuit attached

    Paste coverage under a power device decides the efficiency figure, and X-ray is how that joint gets read.

  • A car amplifier is a power supply with an audio problem, and the yield number is voiding

    A Class-D board is judged on heat before sound, in a box that already runs hot.

  • On a telecom line card the impedance comes from the stackup and the shielding from the assembly floor

    The impedance comes from the stackup and the cans, and assembly is where both can be lost.

Second: the joint that moves

Four families where the failure is mechanical: a connector, a header, a tab, a flex. Assembly executes a drawing here, so the drawing is where the leverage sits.

  • On an AMR the connector fails before the solder joint does

    A robot board is unplugged for a living; mating cycles and strain relief are warranty terms with part numbers.

  • On a receiver card the joint that fails is a ribbon header, three years later, on a wall nobody can reach

    The header is cycled at install, so the joint that fails is three years downstream of the placement.

  • The motor tabs on a power tool controller are soldered, and that is the whole job

    Twenty amps through a soldered tab inside a sealed handle: the through-hole fill and the coating carry field life.

  • On AI glasses the hinge is where the yield goes, and it is a board decision

    The electronics are ordinary; the flex folded every day for two years is where the design gets tested.

Third: the surface

Four families where a coating, a masking or a seal is the specification already, and where applying it late closes the rework path.

  • An off-highway controller is judged in a field six months after it left the line

    Coating inside a casting is judged in a field, six months after the machine left the line.

  • On a rugged handheld the coating is not a finish, it is the specification

    The product survives because of the box and the coat, and the shield can sets the rework path.

  • On a pressure transmitter the op-amp is the easy part and the seal is not

    The op-amp is the easy part; the seal into the housing is the drift figure.

  • A thermal camera core board is a cleaning and masking problem before it is a placement problem

    Flux, dust and an unmasked window ruin a low-noise analogue board before it is tested.

Fourth: the measurement that happens elsewhere

Six families where the acceptance test belongs to another building, so the arrangement for it gets written before the first build.

  • A flight controller is one IMU away from being scrap, and nobody here can tell you which one

    Sensor alignment sits with the aircraft maker, so the boundary is worth drawing on paper.

  • On a GNSS board the RF section is a keep-out, and assembly is what quietly breaks it

    Fifty to five hundred boards a year, an RF front end measured elsewhere, and a coating callout that can detune it.

  • An instrument board is fifty to five hundred pieces, and most of them are a revision

    Bench instruments are analogue, low volume and revision-heavy, and the calibration lives with you.

  • An IP camera board is easy to assemble and hard to prove, because the proof is an image

    When acceptance is an image, the assembler's job is narrow and the proof has to live somewhere else.

  • On a DC fast charger the power modules are bought — the control board is what you are specifying

    The conversion stage arrives as somebody else's certified module; the integration lands on the control board.

  • An induction board is judged at the pan, and the pan is the one thing this floor never connects

    The resonant tank decides the money and the failure, and it lives outside this floor.

Fifth: the record

Four families where the question a buyer ends up answering years later is documentary: which serial, which lot, which revision, which certificate.

  • On a medical device the board is ordinary and the record is not

    An ordinary board with an extraordinary record, in a device that lives a decade.

  • The rack BMS board spends its life at high voltage and nobody ever looks at it

    Spacing, connectors, contactors and records, all judged long after the cabinet shipped.

  • A payment terminal has one trace that must never be nicked, and nothing here can prove it survived

    A silent failure with an audit answer: ask for the audit before you ask for the price.

  • The number a home battery is judged on is measured in microamps, and there is no microamp bench here

    The number the product is judged on is measured in microamps, and half the approval file is generated elsewhere.

Sixth: sameness, and no second attempt

Two families where the requirement is repeatability: one because the unit count is high, one because the board has to work first time.

  • A robot joint board is a circular board built twelve to forty times per machine

    A joint board is built twelve to forty times per unit; panelisation and test coverage decide whether the robot walks smoothly.

  • A wearable board has almost nothing on it and almost no room for a second attempt

    Tiny parts and near-impossible rework: everything gets settled before the build.

If you only read one
A payment terminal has one trace that must never be nicked, and nothing here can prove it survived

Because it is the family where a silent failure has an audit answer, and the note puts the audit before the price — the order most buyers get wrong, and the order that decides whether the rest of this hub is useful to you.
